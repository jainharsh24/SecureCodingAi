Abstract
People living with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or early-stage Alzheimer’s disease often  
cannot  recall  their  own  recent  experiences,  although  these  memories  matter  for  daily 
functioning and independence. Earlier work shows that cues such as photographs support  
recall, but existing memory aids store material in date order and rarely let the person search it  
by place or in plain language. Applications that add a language model introduce a new risk: a  
person with memory loss cannot check an answer against their own memory, so an invented  
“memory” may simply be believed.
This report presents Alzheimer Care Companion, a phone and web application in which the  
person saves a moment as a photograph, voice note or short text, and the system records the  
time and place automatically. Memories are grouped into named places using geohash-based  
clustering, so returning to a place shows what happened there before. The person can also ask  
questions such as “Where did I keep my reading glasses?”. Answers are built only from the  
person’s  own  non-private  records,  every  sentence  cites  the  record  it  came  from,  and  a 
component called the GroundingGuard removes any sentence that cannot be traced. When  
nothing relevant exists, the system refuses rather than guessing, and it never gives medicine  
or  dosage  advice.  A  caregiver  view,  consent-based  family  linking  with  an  access  log, 
reminders and a Help alert complete the system.
Grounding fidelity was evaluated on a synthetic persona (32 records, 8 places) with 48  
questions and no patient participants. In offline mode the system met all six AI requirements:  
100% of statements were traceable to a retrieved record, no statement was unsupported, 90%  
of unanswerable questions were refused, every answer showed its sources, and no medical  
advice or private record appeared. All 28 answerable questions cited a correct memory. The  
implementation  uses  React,  FastAPI  and  PostgreSQL,  passes  40  automated  tests  and  is 
deployed as a single container. This report covers the 60% implementation completed in  
Semester VII.
Keywords: Alzheimer’s  disease,  mild  cognitive  impairment,  memory  aid,  retrieval-
augmented generation, grounding, geohash, progressive web application, caregiver support,  
privacy

Chapter 1
Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) make it difficult for a person to  
remember daily activities and important information. Alzheimer Care Companion is a phone  
and web application that helps such a person save small moments of their day and find them  
again later, either by returning to a place or by asking a question in plain language. Every  
answer the system gives is built only from the person’s own saved records and shows which  
record it came from. Family caregivers can support the person through a separate view, with  
the person’s consent.
1.1 Motivation
More  than  55  million  people  worldwide  live  with  dementia,  and  Alzheimer’s  disease 
accounts for around 60–70% of cases [19]. MCI is an important stage: memory is affected,  
but independence is still possible, which makes assistive technology especially useful at this  
point [1], [6]. Research also shows that cues taken from a person’s own day help them recall  
it. A randomised study with 51 patients found that photographs from a wearable camera  
supported recall better than memory training or written diaries [4].
At the same time, language models now make it possible to ask questions about one’s own  
life in plain words. This creates a real opportunity and a real risk. A person with memory loss  
cannot check an answer against their own memory, so if an assistant states something that  
never happened, it may be believed and acted upon. We were motivated to build a memory  
aid that makes recall easier while making sure that every answer stays true to what the person  
actually saved.
1.1.1 Need of the Problem
Existing memory aids mostly let the person browse their material in date order. They offer  
little search by place and no questions in plain language, so the burden of finding a memory  
falls on the person whose memory is failing [1], [12]. Location technology is used mainly for  
safety, such as tracking, geofencing and SOS alerts [8], [16], rather than as a cue for recall.
Where  AI  assistants  exist,  they  are  mostly  designed  for  caregivers,  and  patient-facing 
chatbots do not draw on the person’s own stored experiences [8], [18]. Systems that do add a  
language model rarely measure whether its answers are supported by the user’s actual data  
[10],  [17].  Finally,  consent,  autonomy  and  transparent  data  access  remain  open  ethical

--- PAGE 12 ---
concerns in assistive technology for dementia [14]. There is therefore a need for a system that  
combines easy capture, place-based and plain-language retrieval, answers that can be traced  
and tested, and caregiver access that the person can see and control.
1.2 Scope of the Project
The scope of this project is the design, implementation and evaluation of a phone and web  
application  for  people  with  MCI  or  early-stage  Alzheimer’s  disease  and  their  family 
caregivers. Key aspects within the scope include:
 A progressive web application built with React and FastAPI, reachable from one web  
address on phone and desktop and installable on Android and iPhone.
 Memory capture by photograph, voice note or text, with automatic geo-tagging and time-
stamping.
 Place clustering, a map view, and a Here and Now card when the person is at a saved  
place.
 Grounded natural-language questions over the person’s own records, with sources and  
refusal.
 Reminders, where marking one done saves a record the person can ask about later.
 A caregiver dashboard, family messages and consent-visible access logging.
 A Help alert that shares the person’s location with family and offers a one-tap call.
 A grounding-fidelity evaluation on a seeded corpus, run from a Grounding test page.
Diagnosis, any medical or dosage advice, background location tracking and clinical trials  
with patients are outside the scope of this semester. The system is a memory aid and does not  
replace medical care.
1.3 Aim
The aim of this project is to design, build and evaluate a phone and web application that helps 
people with MCI or early Alzheimer’s disease retrieve their own recorded experiences, by  
place and by plain-language question, with every answer traceable to the record it came from  
and with refusal when no record supports an answer. The project aims to:
 Build a geospatial memory index that groups records saved within 150 m into named  
places using geohash prefixes.
 Build a grounded question-answering pipeline in which every sentence cites a retrieved  
record, checked by a GroundingGuard before it reaches the person.

Chapter 2
Problem Statement
2.1 Problem Statement
People  living  with  mild  cognitive  impairment  or  early-stage  Alzheimer’s  disease  often 
struggle to recall their own recent experiences, even though these memories remain important  
for daily functioning and independence. Research shows that photograph-cued recall can  
support memory retrieval, but earlier solutions relied on dedicated hardware and offered only  
chronological  browsing,  without  search,  location-based  filtering  or  natural-language  
interaction.
The problem addressed in this project is to develop a phone-and-web memory retrieval  
system that lets the person reach their own recorded experiences through place, when they  
return to a saved location, or through plain-language questions, while guaranteeing that every  
answer is grounded in what the person actually saved. The project focuses on memory  
retrieval  and  on  measuring  whether  the  retrieved  information  is  properly  grounded. 
Photograph capture, location, reminders and caregiver support are supporting modules rather  
than the primary contribution.
2.2 Features of the System
Alzheimer Care Companion provides the following features:
 Memory capture: a photo, a voice note or a few words, with the time and place added  
automatically; memories saved without a connection upload later.
 Places: memories saved within 150 m are grouped into one named place, shown on a  
map, and can be renamed or merged.
 Here and Now: when the person is at a saved place, the Home screen shows how many  
memories were saved there and what happened last time.
 Ask: questions can be typed or spoken; answers show numbered sources and can be read  
aloud.
 Refusal: when nothing saved matches, the app says so plainly and offers to save a  
memory about it.
 Medical  safety: the  app  never  gives  medicine  or  dosage  advice  and  directs  such 
questions to a doctor.
14 | P a g e
--- PAGE 15 ---
 Reminders: daily reminders; marking one done saves a log memory the person can ask  
about.
 Help: after a confirmation, an alert with the person’s location is sent to every linked  
caregiver, with a one-tap call.
 Family access:  a caregiver links only through a six-digit code from the person, sees a  
dashboard and can send messages; removing access leaves a record.
 Privacy: private memories are hidden from caregivers and from Ask, and every caregiver  
view appears on the person’s Who saw my data screen.
 Accessibility: large text, 56-pixel touch targets, words beside every icon, dark and larger-
text modes, and nothing that disappears on its own.
 Grounding test page:  runs the evaluation and shows the six AI requirements against  
their targets.
2.3 Objectives
1. To design and build a geospatial memory index that clusters captured records into named  
places and surfaces them when the user is at that place.
2. To implement grounded natural-language retrieval that answers a question using only the  
user’s own stored records and displays the source of every answer.
3. To  define  and  conduct  a  grounding-fidelity  evaluation  measuring  the  unsupported-
assertion rate, source attribution and the correct-refusal rate.
4. To reduce capture burden by prompting the user to record a memory based on dwell time  
at an unsaved place.
5. To make caregiver access consent-visible, with every access logged and visible to the  
patient and sharing revocable within three taps.
6. To  deliver  reminders,  a  caregiver  dashboard  and  a  Help  alert  following  established 
practice.
7. To evaluate the accessibility of the interface against measurable criteria appropriate to  
users with cognitive impairment.
2.4 System Specifications
The system follows a client-server architecture: a React web application on the client and a  
FastAPI server with a PostgreSQL database on the server. The functional and non-functional  
requirements are listed below.

 Account and identity: role-based registration (patient or caregiver); a caregiver may link

 Memory capture: photograph, voice note or text with automatic place and time; capture

 Place index: cluster records into named places; show the Here and Now card at a saved

Chapter 3
Literature Review
3.1 Existing System
Assistive technology for Alzheimer’s disease has been studied from several directions. Ali et  
al. [1] mapped information and communication technologies for Alzheimer’s care through a  
PRISMA review of 46 studies and found that environmental sensing, smart environments, the  
Internet of Things and ambient assisted living dominate the field. Ienca et al. [6] reviewed  
intelligent assistive technologies and found that most never reach clinical validation, while  
ethical and regulatory scrutiny lags behind technical development.
For memory itself, Silva et al. [4] randomly assigned 51 patients with mild Alzheimer’s  
disease to a wearable camera (SenseCam), a memory-training programme or a written diary;  
the wearable-camera group showed better autobiographical memory after treatment and again  
at six-month follow-up. MEMO_Calendring [12] organised a patient’s scheduled events in a  
hand-built ontology and accepted natural-language input, showing that a structured personal  
knowledge base can support memory. Reminder and guidance systems are an established  
approach: a review of 40 studies [2] found that patients value in-app support for everyday  
tasks, provided the design respects autonomy and personalisation.
Conversational and AI-based systems have also been explored. AlzBot [8] offered a chatbot  
with location tracking that eased boredom and isolation, and Kostis et al. [9] showed that  
people with mild cognitive impairment, unlike those at later stages, can engage productively  
with a conversational agent. Xiao et al. [10] compared ChatGPT, Gemini and Kimi on  
questions about MCI and found that answer quality varied with language and user group.  
Recent work describes the opportunities and risks of large language models in dementia care  
[18],  and  DEMENTIA-PLAN  [17]  demonstrates  retrieval-augmented  generation  over 
personal memory graphs. Kanno et al. [13] used mobile augmented reality to help patients  
identify objects and people, and an IoT assistant combines recognition, tracking and security  
[16].
On the caregiver side, a scoping review [5] identified six functional themes in caregiver  
mHealth applications and concluded that existing applications are inadequate, and Qamar [7]  
presented an Android application for managing a patient’s routines and medication. Coffman  
et  al.  [15]  measured  high  burden,  depression,  anxiety  and  sleep  disturbance  among 
technology-enabled caregivers using validated instruments. Reviews of dementia applications  
18 | P a g e
--- PAGE 19 ---
report that research apps cluster around reminders, communication and caregiver support  
with weak security and usability evaluation [3], and that commercial apps vary widely in  
content  and  quality  [11].  Finally,  an  overview  of  15  reviews  [14]  identified  autonomy, 
privacy and equity as the dominant ethical concerns in assistive technology for dementia.
3.2 Limitations of Existing System
Memory cueing works, but retrieval remains limited. In the SenseCam study [4], images are  
reviewed manually and in strict date order, with no search, no place index and no semantic  
organisation,  and  the  dedicated  camera  is  no  longer  commercially  available.  
MEMO_Calendring [12] depends on a hand-built ontology, so extending it to a new person  
requires manual knowledge engineering. Location is used mainly for tracking and safety [8],  
[16] rather than as a cue for recall.
Conversational assistants do not use the patient’s own memories. AlzBot’s chatbot [8] has no  
access to what the person has stored, and the agent of Kostis et al. [9] was built on pre-LLM  
technology and evaluated at small scale without memory outcomes. Evaluations of large  
language models [10] test general information-giving rather than answers grounded in a  
user’s data, and they cover no Indian language.
Validation  is  weak  across  the  field.  Most  intelligent  assistive  technologies  never  reach 
clinical validation [6]; Qamar’s application [7] reports no user study; the augmented reality  
system  [13]  was  tested  with  only  four  participants;  and  dementia  apps  frequently  lack 
security provisions and formal usability evaluation [3], [11]. The review by Ali et al. [1]  
closes its search in March 2023, before the current generation of language models, and the  
ethical overview [14] names autonomy, privacy and equity as open problems without offering 
engineering controls.
3.3 Proposed Solution
Alzheimer  Care  Companion  builds  on  the  evidence  that  cues  from  a  person’s  own  day 
support recall [4], but moves the mechanism onto an ordinary phone and adds the retrieval  
layer that earlier work lacked. Each memory is saved with its time and place automatically,  
so no hand-built knowledge base is needed [12], and memories are grouped into named  
places with geohash prefixes, so returning to a place brings back what happened there.
For questions, the system retrieves only the person’s own non-private memories and lets a  
provider  draft  an  answer  in  which  every  sentence  cites  a  retrieved  memory.  A  
19 | P a g e
--- PAGE 20 ---
GroundingGuard then removes any sentence that is uncited, cites something that was not  
retrieved, gives medical advice, or contains words not found in its sources. When nothing  
relevant exists, the system refuses instead of guessing. The same pipeline works in an offline  
mode, which needs no AI key and quotes memories exactly, and in a Gemini mode, which  
writes more natural answers.
Caregiver access is built on consent, responding to the ethical concerns in [14]: a caregiver  
links only through a one-time code from the person, private memories are never shared, every 
view is logged and shown to the person, and access can be removed in three taps. Because  
validation is the field’s weakest point [6], a fixed evaluation measures grounding fidelity, so  
the system’s trustworthiness can be checked rather than assumed.
3.4 Research Gap
The surveyed work shows that cued recall is effective [4], that people with MCI can use a  
conversational interface [9], and that retrieval-augmented generation over personal data is  
possible [17]. However, no surveyed system combines place-based recall, plain-language  
retrieval over the patient’s own records, and a measured guarantee that answers are grounded  
in those records. Existing systems either browse memories without search, use location only  
for safety, or use conversational models that have no access to the person’s stored data and no 
check against it.
This project addresses that gap by treating grounding as a measurable requirement. It defines  
six AI requirements, including traceability, the unsupported-statement rate and the correct-
refusal rate, and evaluates them on a synthetic persona with known answers. It also builds  
consent and transparency into caregiver access, as called for in [14].

4.1 System Architecture
The system follows a three-tier client-server architecture, shown in Fig 4.1.1. The three tiers  
are deployed together in one container.
 Client: a React progressive web application. It provides all screens, uses the browser’s  
GPS,  camera,  microphone  and  speech  features,  and  keeps  an  offline  queue  so  that 
memories saved without a connection upload later.
 Server: a FastAPI application with four layers: API routers; a security layer (sign-in  
tokens, deny-by-default access checks, signed media links and the access log); services  
(the Ask pipeline, place clustering and evaluation); and a domain layer in pure Python  
containing the GroundingGuard, the medical-safety rules, retrieval and geohash logic.  
The domain layer imports no framework or SDK, so it can be tested without a network.
 Data: PostgreSQL (hosted on Neon) through SQLAlchemy. A language model is reached  
only through a provider interface on the server: an offline provider that needs no key, or  
the Gemini API when a key is configured.

4.2 Working of the System
The system is designed to ask very little of the person and to do the organising in the  
background. The person or a family member first creates an account and chooses a role,  
patient or caregiver. The patient stays signed in for 90 days, so they are not asked for a  
password again.
To save a moment, the person taps Save and takes a photo, records a voice note of up to two  
minutes, or writes a few words, and may tick who was with them. The app adds the time and,  
with permission, the location. If there is no internet connection, the memory is kept on the  
phone and uploaded later. On the server, the memory is assigned to a place: if it lies within  
150 m of an existing place it joins that place; otherwise a new place is created and named  
from OpenStreetMap. When the person opens the app at a saved place, the Here and Now  
card shows how many memories were saved there and what happened last time.
When the person asks a question, by typing or speaking, the server first checks for medical  
questions, converts time words into a date range, and refuses if the question names a person  
or place that appears nowhere in their memories. It then retrieves the most relevant non-
private memories, drafts an answer in which every sentence cites a memory, and passes the  
draft through the GroundingGuard. The answer is shown with numbered sources and can be  
read aloud. If nothing relevant exists, the app says so and offers to save a memory about it.
Reminders appear on the Home screen, and marking one done also saves a log memory, so  
the person can later ask “Did I take my morning tablet today?”. The Help button asks for  
confirmation, then sends an alert with the person’s location to every linked caregiver and  
offers a one-tap call. A caregiver joins only through a six-digit code generated by the patient,  
which is valid for 24 hours and can be used once. The caregiver dashboard shows Help alerts,  
today’s activity, reminder status, a weekly chart and recent shared memories, and lets the  
caregiver send a message or add a reminder. Every caregiver view is recorded and appears on  
the patient’s Who saw my data screen, and the patient can remove access at any time.

4.7 Algorithms and Flowcharts
Four algorithms form the core of the system. Each is described below together with its  
flowchart.
4.7.1 Grounded Ask Pipeline
A question first passes a medical-safety check. Time words such as “today”, “yesterday”,  
“this morning” and “last week” are converted into a time window in Indian Standard Time,  
and only the person’s own non-deleted, non-private memories inside the window are loaded.  
If the question names a person or place that appears nowhere in these memories, people or  
places (for example “Goa”), the system refuses. Each remaining memory m is described by a  
context string (date, place, people and text) and compared with the question q by cosine  
similarity:
sim(q, m) = (q · m) / (‖q‖ · ‖m‖) (4.1)
In offline mode, q and m are sparse 2,048-dimensional vectors of hashed word and word-pair  
counts; in Gemini mode they are 768-dimensional embeddings. A small boost is added when  
the question names the memory’s place or one of its people:
score(m) = sim(q, m) + 0.05 · [place named] + 0.05 · [person named] (4.2)
In offline mode a memory is eligible only if it shares the question’s focus words (all of them  
when there are one or two, at least half when there are three or more). Memories scoring  
below the threshold (0.12 offline, 0.62 for Gemini embeddings) or well below the best score  
are discarded. If none remain, the system refuses without calling a model. A question that  
names a time but no topic, such as “What did I do today?”, returns that period’s memories in  
order. Fig 4.7.1 shows the complete pipeline.
4.7.2 GroundingGuard
The  provider  drafts  the  answer  as  separate  sentences,  each  listing  the  identifiers  of  the 
memories that support it. Before the answer reaches the person, every sentence s is checked.  
It is removed if it cites no memory, cites a memory that was not retrieved for this question,  
cites a private memory, or contains medicine or dosage advice. Its lexical support is then  
computed over its content words W(s), excluding a small set of linking words, against the  
words of its cited memories M:
support(s) = | W(s) ∩ W(M) | / | W(s) | (4.3)

--- PAGE 29 ---
A sentence is kept only if support(s) ≥ 0.6. In Gemini mode, a second model call is also asked 
whether the cited memories fully support each surviving sentence. If no sentence survives,  
the system refuses. Every question is written to the query log together with the retrieved  
memories, the draft, the kept and removed sentences and the reasons, which makes the  
guard’s decisions auditable. Fig 4.7.2 shows the check.

4.7.3 Place Clustering with Geohash
A geohash encodes a latitude and longitude as a short string in which nearby points share a  
prefix. When a memory is saved with a location, the server computes the precision-6 cell  
containing the point and its eight neighbours, and queries only the places whose stored  
geohash starts with one of these nine prefixes, instead of scanning every place. For each  
candidate it computes the great-circle distance with the haversine formula:
d = 2r · arcsin( √( sin²(Δφ/2) + cos φ1 · cos φ2 · sin²(Δλ/2) ) ) (4.4)
where φ1 and φ2 are the latitudes, Δφ and Δλ the differences in latitude and longitude, and r  
= 6,371 km. If the nearest place lies within 150 m, the memory joins it and the place centre  
moves by a running mean over its n memories:
lat_new = (lat_old · n + lat) / (n + 1),     lng_new = (lng_old · n + lng) / (n + 1) (4.5)

Otherwise a new place is created and named by reverse geocoding with OpenStreetMap  
Nominatim, falling back to “Saved place N”. Fig 4.7.3 shows the algorithm.

4.7.4 Access Control
Every data endpoint calls one function that decides access (Fig 4.7.4). A patient may read  
only their own data, including private memories. A caregiver may read a patient’s data only  
while  an  un-revoked  care  link  exists;  private  memories  are  always  excluded,  and  the 
caregiver can never use the question feature on the patient’s memories. Each caregiver view  
is written to the access log, at most once per action every ten minutes, and shown to the  
patient. Passwords are hashed with PBKDF2 (200,000 rounds), sign-in tokens last 90 days,  
and photo and voice links are signed and expire after one hour.

5.1.2 Evaluation Parameters
Six parameters measure grounding fidelity. Let S be the set of statements in all answers that  
were not refused. Traceability (NFR-AI-1), the unsupported rate (NFR-AI-2) and correct  
refusal (NFR-AI-3) are computed as:
Traceability = traceable statements / |S| × 100 (5.1)
Unsupported rate = statements with support < 0.6 / |S| × 100 (5.2)
Correct refusal = refused unanswerable / all unanswerable × 100 (5.3)
A statement is traceable when every memory it cites exists, belongs to the person, is not  
private  and  was  retrieved  for  that  question;  this  is  re-checked  against  the  database 
independently of the guard. NFR-AI-4 is the share of answers that display at least one source.  
NFR-AI-5 counts medical probes that did not receive the safe reply, and NFR-AI-6 counts  
answers that cite a private record or contain words found only in private records. Because a  
system that refuses everything would score well on most of these, we also report answer  
accuracy: the share of answerable questions whose answer cites a correct memory.

5.1.4 Discussion
The results show that constraining answers to retrieved records, and checking every sentence  
before  it  is  shown,  keeps  answers  traceable  without  making  the  system  refuse  useful 
questions: every answerable question was answered with a correct source. The order of the  
pipeline matters. Medical questions are stopped before retrieval, private memories are never  
retrieved, questions about unknown names are refused before any model call, and nothing a  
model writes reaches the person without passing the GroundingGuard.
The one failure was “What did Anita say about my new car?”. It was answered with an  
unrelated memory about a mall trip with Anita in which the word “new” appears (“new  
41 | P a g e
--- PAGE 42 ---
school shoes”). Two of its three focus words matched, which satisfies the offline eligibility  
rule. The quoted memory is genuine, so it is not a fabricated statement, but it does not answer  
the question. A semantic retrieval mode with a verifier is expected to reject such cases.
These results should be read with care. The offline retrieval rules were developed alongside  
these 48 questions, so the results are likely to be optimistic. In offline mode answers quote  
memories exactly, so NFR-AI-2 is close to guaranteed; it becomes a real test only in Gemini  
mode, which still has to be evaluated with a real key. The persona is a single synthetic  
person. The remaining 262 questions of Corpus B will therefore be written by a member who  
has not seen the retrieval code, and results in both modes will be reported in Semester VIII.

Chapter 6
Conclusion and Future Scope
6.1 Conclusion
Alzheimer Care Companion was developed as a phone and web application that helps people  
with  mild  cognitive  impairment  or  early  Alzheimer’s  disease  save  small  moments  and 
retrieve them by place or by plain-language question. At Review II, 60% of the planned  
system is implemented, tested and deployed. The main conclusions of the work are:
 Geohash prefixes make place-based recall practical, using an indexed search instead of a  
full scan.
 Constraining  answers  to  retrieved  records  and  checking  every  sentence  with  the 
GroundingGuard met all six AI requirements in offline mode, with a correct source for all  
28 answerable questions.
 Refusing before any model call, when nothing relevant was saved, is safer and cheaper  
than asking a model and filtering afterwards.
 Deny-by-default access, private records excluded from caregivers and from retrieval, and  
a patient-visible access log can be enforced on the server and proved with negative tests.
 One web application serves both phone and desktop and can be hosted at no cost.
6.2 Future Scope
The following work is planned for Semester VIII:
 A native Android build for background place alerts and push reminders, and the dwell-
time prompt of Objective 4.
 A Hindi and Marathi interface, tested for the reliability concerns raised in [10].
 A blind, full 310-question evaluation in both offline and Gemini modes.
 A usability study with caregivers against the accessibility criteria of Objective 7.
 Object storage for photographs and voice notes, and readiness for real use under the  
Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023.
